Acoustic systems analysis · loudspeaker directivity

Why four horns don’t make four times the megaphone

Four directional horns splayed 90° apart for 360° coverage look like four times the system. The arrangement spends the horns’ one real asset — directivity — to buy angular coverage, then under-delivers that coverage in exactly the octaves that carry consonants. The defect is invisible to the measurement everyone takes.

01PREMISE

The apparent advantage

Three of the four reasons to like this design are sound. The fourth is the one everybody actually buys it for.

A horn is an acoustic transformer and a directivity device at once. A representative re-entrant paging horn reaches about 110 dB SPL at 1 W / 1 m on axis, against roughly 88–92 dB for a direct-radiating cone of similar size — a 20 dB advantage, part genuine efficiency, part concentration into a restricted solid angle.

Q ≈ 41253 / (θh × θv) = 41253 / (60 × 40) = 17.2 → DI ≈ 12.4 dB

That 12.4 dB is the asset. Four such horns tile the whole circle, so no listener sits behind the system and the aiming problem disappears; each driver runs at a quarter power, which helps thermal compression and distortion. All true. The claim that does not survive is the implied one: that four horns are therefore louder.

02POWER

Four horns produce no extra level anywhere

Total radiated acoustic power and sound pressure at a listener are different quantities. Adding horns raises the first and leaves the second alone, because each new horn radiates into a different solid angle. Stand in front of horn 1 and you receive essentially all your signal from horn 1: horns 2 and 4 are 90° off their own axes as seen from you, horn 3 points away. Their power is, from your position, discarded.

Doubling pressure at a point needs coherent summation at that point — two sources arriving at comparable level, in phase. That requires them aimed at the same place. Horns 90° apart are, by construction, doing the opposite.

The same fact reads a second way, as directivity. Four 60°×40° horns tiled without gaps span 240°×40°, giving Q = 4.30 and DI = 6.3 dB — precisely 17.2/4. Splaying horns converts directivity into coverage, one‑for‑one. The 6.1 dB of DI thrown away and the 6.0 dB of SPL lost are the same event described twice.

And efficiency is not the problem

Working back from 110 dB/W/m at Q = 17.2: 0.071 W acoustic from 1 W electrical, about 7% efficient, against 0.5–2% for a direct radiator. Efficiency is the horn’s strength. Keep it in proportion for what follows — halving it would cost 3 dB, and the next section finds a 27 dB problem.

Verdict on power

The system is not short of acoustic power. The power accounting is wrong — there is no 4× gain, there is a 6 dB loss per direction — but raw output is rarely the binding constraint.

03COVERAGE

The coverage it buys is frequency-dependent

Four horns at 90° spacing cover the circle uniformly only if each horn’s −6 dB beamwidth is at least 90°. That is the classical −6 dB crossover criterion: adjacent sources each 6 dB down sum to 3 dB down, giving ±3 dB of ripple around the circle.

But horn beamwidth is strongly frequency-dependent. A horn is near-omnidirectional below the frequency at which its mouth becomes acoustically large, and narrows steadily above it — narrowing fastest exactly where intelligibility lives.

Nominal 360° versus uniform 360°

The system does radiate into all 360°. Every azimuth receives sound. It is still not a 360° system in any useful sense, and the gap shows up as a disagreement between instruments pointed at the same spot:

The central result

The coverage seam is not a level defect. It is a spectral defect that a level measurement cannot see. The system is loud and unintelligible at the same position — and loudness is what gets measured.

Consequence for the listener: walking around the system changes the timbre, not the volume. It gets duller and brighter rather than louder and quieter. Reported as “muffled in places”, diagnosed as a power problem, treated by turning it up — which does nothing at all.

04INTERACTION

Interference: real, stable, and secondary

The horns share a signal, so they are coherent sources and they interfere where their patterns overlap. The honest scale of this effect is smaller than §03 — and it is worth being precise about where it applies, because it is the effect most often overstated.

The structural tension

Narrow horns give small overlap zones — little interference, but large coverage gaps. Wide horns give smooth level coverage — but large overlap zones, and therefore combing. Splaying horns cannot escape this. You only choose which artefact you get.

On the exact bisector between two horns the mouths are equidistant, so the arrivals add in phase: +6 dB relative to one source, not the +3 dB an incoherent estimate predicts. Coherence helps on the seam centre. Move off it by angle φ and the path difference is Δ ≈ d sin φ. For a compact head with mouth-centre radius 0.2 m, adjacent separation d = 0.283 m:

Below c/2d ≈ 606 Hz no destructive null can exist anywhere: the sources are closer than half a wavelength and behave as one. That splits the array’s real behaviour in two — and produces the design’s quiet irony.

Below ~600 Hz the four horns are acoustically one source. They cannot be steered and cannot interfere, and the splayed geometry contributes nothing: four amplifier channels are being spent to produce an omnidirectional low-frequency field. Above ~1.5 kHz the geometry is under-populated and coverage breaks into petals. The arrangement is well matched to the octave around 1 kHz, and only that octave — one octave of a five-octave speech signal.

Why it does not average out with distance — and why it is still not the main problem

Beyond the array near field (R ≳ 2d²/λ ≈ 1.9 m at 4 kHz) the pattern becomes a fixed function of angle. It does not wash out: a listener at 50 m in a null direction is in the same null as one at 20 m. You cannot get far enough away to escape a comb.

What limits its importance is spacing in frequency, Δf = c/Δ. The array’s own path differences are ≤ 0.3 m, so its combs are spaced 1 kHz and wider — broader than the ear’s ⅓-octave filters, so they are heard as a change in tone colour, not as comb filtering. The dense, intelligibility-destroying combs need path differences of metres, which the array cannot produce. Those come from the environment.

05ENVIRONMENT

The room takes the rest

Everything so far assumed free field. Real sites attack what directivity the array has left.

Ground reflection: a comb that sweeps

Source at 4 m, ear at 1.6 m, over reflective ground:

Surfaces, and a 360° pattern’s unique exposure

A facade 20 m behind the listener adds up to 40 m of path — about 117 ms. Speech integrates reflections usefully only within roughly the first 50 ms; beyond that, a reflection within ~10 dB of the direct sound is a discrete echo that smears syllables. An aimed horn can be pointed away from a hard facade. A four-horn array radiates a full quarter of its power into every surface around it, by design. The 360° pattern is not merely neutral toward reflective surfaces — it feeds them.

Reverberation: high SPL, low intelligibility

Illustrative concourse: V = 20,000 m³, S = 6,000 m², α = 0.15 → room constant 1059 m², RT60 = 3.6 s.

Dc = 0.141 √(Q · R) → one aimed horn 19.0 m · four-horn array 9.5 m

Exactly half — the √4 that follows from the DI given away in §02. Beyond Dc the reverberant field dominates: SPL stops falling while the direct-to-reverberant ratio keeps degrading. Peutz articulation loss, %ALcons ≈ 200 r²RT²/(V·Q), capped at 9·RT60 beyond 3.16 Dc:

The saturation trap

Beyond 3.16 Dc, articulation loss depends only on RT60 — not on distance, not on Q, not on level. Turning the amplifier up raises the direct and reverberant fields equally, leaving D/R unchanged. More power just makes the unintelligible louder.

And a final pathology: reverberation is diffuse, so it fills the seam on the meter. Measured SPL at the 45° position looks almost normal, while the missing direct high-frequency energy has been replaced by time-smeared reverberant energy — worse than silence in that band. The measurement improves as the listening gets worse.

06DIAGNOSIS

Ranking the causes

Theory says: four horns → four directions → 360° coverage → high output. Reality runs: power split four ways (−6 dB) → DI quartered → beamwidth narrows with frequency, so 90° spacing under-covers above ~1.5 kHz → spectral seams every 90° → lobing in the overlaps → a quarter of the power into every surface → critical distance halved → high SPL, collapsed articulation.

Root cause

Coverage non-uniformity driven by the frequency-dependence of horn directivity, with reverberation as its amplifier in real rooms. Both descend from one act: splaying the horns spends the array’s directivity on angular coverage. That single decision produces the seams and the reverberation vulnerability.

Note which two candidates the intuitive analysis worries about — power and efficiency. They are the two that finish last. That is why this failure is almost always misdiagnosed.

08CONCLUSION

Why it underperforms its own configuration

The answer

Splaying four horns spends the horns’ only real asset — directivity — on angular coverage, then delivers that coverage non-uniformly across frequency, in an environment that punishes the lost directivity a second time.

1. No extra level anywhere. With a fixed amplifier, four directions cost 10 log10(4) = 6 dB in every one of them, because pressure at a point is set by the one or two horns aimed at it, not by total radiated power.

2. The coverage under-delivers where speech lives. 90° spacing needs ≥ 90° beamwidth; real horns give that at 1 kHz and about 40° at 4 kHz. The seams are therefore low-pass filters, not level dips.

3. The dominant failure is invisible to the dominant measurement. A broadband or A-weighted survey reports a few decibels of ripple and passes the system. Only band-resolved SPL or STI/STIPA reveals it. Hence “muffled in places”, treated as a power problem, forever.

4. Reverberation converts the sacrificed directivity into an intelligibility failure, then saturates. Quartering Q halves Dc; %ALcons at 30 m goes 6.7% → 26.8% on identical hardware; and beyond 3.16 Dc no amount of amplifier power can improve intelligibility at all.

Inter-horn interference and horn efficiency are real but secondary — and they are exactly what the intuitive analysis worries about.

Design changes, ranked by benefit per unit of effort

Tier 1 · addresses the root cause

Largest available improvement. Everything else is refinement.

  1. Match beamwidth to spacing at the highest frequency that matters, not the lowest. Specify constant-directivity horns holding a nominal 90° pattern to 6–8 kHz, or raise the horn count to match the high-frequency beamwidth — six at 60°, about nine at 40°. Recovers the entire 10–27 dB deficit.
  2. Question whether 360° is actually required. It usually is not: some sectors face water, fields, a hillside. Serving 180° with two horns instead of 360° with four returns +3 dB of level and +3 dB of DI for free, halves the seams, and stops radiating into two reflective quadrants. The cheapest gain in the system.
  3. Split by frequency band instead of by mechanical splay. Below ~600–800 Hz the horns are omnidirectional and interfering to no purpose. Build one low-frequency section at full power crossed over at 0.8–1.2 kHz to pattern-controlled HF devices.

Tier 2 · significant, moderate cost

  1. Individual amplification and per-horn DSP. Removes the 2 Ω parallel-load problem, and allows per-sector level shading for different throws, per-sector HF shelving, and muting of unused directions.
  2. Crossover and EQ aimed at the speech band. Band-limit to ~250 Hz–5 kHz; a 3–6 dB presence lift at 2–4 kHz buys consonants directly. In noisy or reverberant sites this buys more STI than another 3 dB of raw SPL — and unlike raw SPL it still works past Dc.
  3. Orientation and geometry. Down-tilt 10–20° so the main lobe meets the listener plane instead of overshooting into facades; align all four horns to a common acoustic origin, the one change that genuinely reduces overlap-zone lobing.

Tier 3 · limited for a compact array

  1. Delay / phase optimisation. With all mouths inside a ~0.3 m circle, delay can shift the interference structure but cannot create high-frequency energy no horn is radiating in that direction. A seam from insufficient beamwidth is not a phase problem.
  2. Sequential sector firing. Round-robin at full power gives +6 dB per sector with zero interference — genuinely useful for sirens and tone alerts, but it fragments live speech.

Tier 4 · replace the architecture

If the requirement is genuinely uniform 360° speech coverage, splayed horns are the wrong tool.

  1. Vertical line array / column. Omnidirectional in azimuth — no seams and no horizontal inter-source interference at all — while concentrating directivity in the vertical plane. This is the architecturally correct answer: it puts Q where it is useful, suppressing ground and ceiling reflections and raising Dc, while serving the listener plane uniformly. Solves §03, §04 and §05 at once.
  2. Toroidal / radial “donut” horn. One driver into a circumferential 360° flare: seamless, single coherent source. Vertical Q is limited, but a large improvement on four splayed horns.
  3. Distributed system. Several lower-power units near their listeners. Halving distance is worth 6 dB, and decisively, every listener sits nearer some source than the reverberant field, so D/R improves everywhere at once. In reverberant or built-up sites this beats any single-point architecture, including a perfect one.
  4. Steerable beam-forming column. For difficult indoor sites, aiming the vertical beam at the audience plane and away from hard surfaces electronically.
09VERIFY

Replacing estimates with data

The single most diagnostic test

Measure broadband SPL and 4 kHz octave-band SPL at the same positions around the circle, and plot both. If the result is a flat broadband curve over a scalloped high-frequency curve, the problem is beam coverage — and no amplifier will fix it.
